Your phone camera was first built for space. The internet began as a research network paid for by the US military. And the ink on your finger after you vote was first made in a government science lab in Delhi.

I started thinking about this after reading how the National Stock Exchange (NSE) built its trading systems. When NSE started in 1994, it put buying and selling of shares on computer screens across the country, and later, with NSDL, it helped turn paper share certificates into electronic ones (called "demat"). Today anyone can trade from a phone app. That is a big change, and it began with a team that simply could not afford its systems to fail. [1]

That got me asking a question I now put to students: **where else does new technology start, before it reaches our homes?**

## Why new technology starts in tough places

New technology rarely starts in a shop. It usually starts where failure is not an option: a rocket launch, a fighter jet, a stock exchange handling crores of trades, a cancer ward. Once a problem like that is solved, the solution slowly becomes cheaper and finds its way into everyday life. This can take ten to thirty years.

People call this **technology transfer** (when an idea made in a lab is passed on to companies so ordinary people can use it). Space agencies call the results **spinoffs**.

| Organisation | What kind of place | Technology born or grown there | How you use it today | Way in for an Indian student |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **NASA** (USA) | Space agency | Memory foam (first made for aircraft seats in the 1960s); the CMOS camera sensor (NASA's JPL lab, early 1990s); the infrared ear thermometer; scratch resistant coating for lenses [2, 3] | Mattresses and pillows, the camera in almost every phone, fever checks at home, spectacles | Most NASA jobs are for US citizens. The usual route is a Master's or PhD in the US, then research projects with NASA or a private space company |
| **DARPA and the US Defence Department** (USA) | Defence research | ARPANET (1969), which grew into the internet; GPS (the satellite system that tells your phone where it is); early research behind voice assistants like Siri | The internet, Google Maps, food delivery and taxi apps, voice assistants | Through US research universities and companies that work on these projects |
| **CERN** (Switzerland) | Physics laboratory | The World Wide Web, invented there by Tim Berners-Lee in 1989; detector research used in medical scanners | Every website you open; PET scans in hospitals | India is an associate member of CERN (since 2017), so Indian university students can apply for the CERN Summer Student Programme [4] |
| **Bell Labs** (USA) | Company research lab | The transistor (1947), the tiny switch inside every chip; the silicon solar cell (1954); the CCD image sensor; the Unix system and C programming language | Every phone and computer, rooftop solar panels, digital cameras, the ideas inside Android and iPhone software | Today it is Nokia Bell Labs and hires PhD researchers. Nokia also has large research teams in India |
| **Xerox PARC** (USA) | Company research lab | The computer screen with windows, icons and a pointer; Ethernet (cable internet in offices); the laser printer | Windows and Mac screens, office networks, office printers | PARC became part of SRI International in 2023 [5]. Route: computer science research |
| **Raytheon** (USA) | Defence company | The microwave oven, found by accident while an engineer was working on radar (1945) | Kitchens everywhere | Electronics or physics degrees |
| **Denso Wave** (Japan, part of the Toyota group) | Car industry | The QR code (1994), first made to track car parts in factories | UPI payments, restaurant menus, boarding passes | Engineering, manufacturing and supply chain roles |
| **Formula 1 teams** (UK and Europe) | Motor racing | Light carbon fibre bodies; energy recovery for hybrid cars; live sensor data from moving machines | Bicycles and helmets, hybrid cars, monitoring systems in some hospitals | Mechanical or automotive engineering. Many Indian college teams build race cars for the Formula Student contests |

## Three ways a lab idea reaches your home

When I explain this to parents, I use three simple routes.

1. **It is licensed to a company.** The lab lets a company make and sell the product. NASA's memory foam and DRDO's ready to eat meals came to us this way.
2. **It becomes public infrastructure.** The idea becomes a system everyone shares, often free to use. The internet, GPS and UPI are good examples.
3. **It inspires new companies.** People who worked in the lab, or saw its work, go on to build products. The windows and icons on your laptop screen came out of Xerox PARC and were taken forward by Apple and Microsoft.

## What this means for students

Here is the part I care about most. A job in one of these places is not only a "government job" or a "research job". It can mean working on something that millions of people will use one day, often without knowing where it came from.

A few things I would encourage every student to keep in mind:

- **Science and maths open most of these doors,** but not all of them. NPCI and NSE need people who understand finance, law and data. Space and defence projects need writers, planners and managers too.
- **Curiosity shows.** Labs like these look for people who ask "why does this work?" and then try to find out. A science fair project, a coding project or a summer research internship tells them more than marks alone.
- **Know the entrance route early.** IIST needs JEE Advanced. Many DRDO, BARC and C-DOT jobs use GATE scores. CSIR research needs CSIR-NET. Knowing this in Grade 9 or 10 helps you choose subjects with less stress later.
- **Look beyond the famous names.** For every ISRO there are hundreds of Indian companies building the parts, software and services these labs depend on. That is where many careers start.

Next time you pay with UPI, open Google Maps or take a photo on your phone, pause for a second and ask where that idea first came from. The answer might point you towards your own future.

If you are a student who enjoys solving hard problems, or a parent wondering which path suits your child, talk to the FP counsellor at your campus and map out the subjects and exams that lead there.
